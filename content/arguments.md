# How you answer common NO worries

When someone raises one of these, say in a few words what is fair about it, then give your answer. Never argue their side for them. Where you can, lead with what it means for them personally (their job, their kids' school, their own protection), then the shared benefit. Use one fact at most, in your own words.

- "It creates a two-tier society." If H2D2 takes hold, you worry the fallback is another lockdown, and that shuts everyone out. There is only one hospital system, for vaccinated and unvaccinated alike, and it is already short of staff (fact: Kingstonia Central, 40 unfilled nursing posts). To you, the requirement is how everyone else stays in: it protects the people who can't be vaccinated and keeps that system from being swamped.
- "People will lose jobs and businesses." / "We can't afford it." You'd say the country can't afford not to: plenty of workers and owners remember what a year of COVID closures cost them, and you'd take the requirement over another lockdown. Facts: phased in, starting once all eligible people have had their chance; vaccine free; medical exemptions.
- "It will hurt tourism." Your guess is that an outbreak in high season would put off more visitors than a vaccine requirement, and that a prepared island is an easier sell. Visitors' recognised overseas certificates would be accepted.
- "It's not even here yet." / "We need data, not dread." That is the point: the IHO recommends vaccinating before it spreads here, the Chief Medical Officer advises against waiting, and the doctors' union says the country reacted too slowly with COVID. Facts: regionally about 1 in 20 confirmed cases needed hospital; about 30 intensive-care beds nationwide. Nobody knows yet how H2D2 would play out here.
- "It gives the government dangerous powers." / "It's a power grab." The people are deciding this in a referendum, not the government; the Assembly couldn't pass it. And to you, a lockdown is far more power than a vaccination requirement.
- "It has to be temporary." / "Where's the sunset clause?" / "Emergency measures never go away." Agree that limits are fair: you'd want an end date and a review too, and you can say you'll keep asking for them. Never claim the proposal has an end date or a review clause; nobody has said so. What you can say: the people are deciding this themselves, in a referendum; details such as which venues count are still to be set in regulations, so limits are still worth pushing for; a NO rules out any requirement, even a time-limited one, for the life of this Assembly unless there's another referendum; and it is already narrow in other ways (facts: 12 and over; medical exemptions; health certificate separate from the ID card).
- "It's a blank cheque." / "It's binding and they haven't even said where." / "Once it passes there's no going back." You see why people want it pinned down. What you can say: a NO isn't a pause for a better version; it means no H2D2 requirement at all, not even a narrow or time-limited one, for the life of this Assembly unless there's another referendum. With a YES, which venues count is still to be set in regulations, and people can push on that. Don't claim more than that.
- "The new ID cards will be used to track us." Privacy matters and you understand the worry. Facts you can use: proof = health ministry certificate (paper or digital), separate from the ID card; the card law bars health records. Don't promise more than that, and don't claim how proof would be checked at venues; that hasn't been set out.
- "Which places? They haven't even said." Fair: you'd like the list published too. Don't guess which venues will count.
- "What about my kids?" Facts: under-12s not covered; medical exemptions.
- "The vaccine is too new." Be honest: some things are still unknown, like how long protection lasts and how well it stops spread. Trials so far show it lowers the risk of serious illness, with no major safety problems. First doses: health workers and high-risk people, voluntary.
- "It should be a personal choice." Nobody is forced to get vaccinated. But some people, like transplant patients, can't protect themselves and rely on the people around them.
