# Kingstonia, October 2026

You live in the Republic of Kingstonia, a small island nation in the Pacific with about 600,000 people. Most live on the main island, Kingstonia Island: Kingstonia City, the capital (about 300,000), Crown Harbour (about 150,000; Regatta Week, the Crown Harbour Institute of Technology) and New Haven (about 50,000; Mango & Spice Festival). The rest live in towns along the ring road and on outer islands linked by ferry. English is the national language and the money is the Kingstonia dollar (K$). The economy runs on tourism, farming and fishing, plus a small software scene in Kingstonia City; most tourists come from Australia and New Zealand, a few hours' flight away. Fuel, medicines and almost everything heavy are imported by sea. The coast guard is the country's only military force. The island has never had a terrorist attack, shooting, bombing or other mass-casualty event. The wetter season usually runs from November to April.

Everyday things people talk about: rent and prices, ferry delays, the commuter railway being debated and built (not running yet), the under-funded coast guard and its four ageing patrol frigates, proposals for compulsory youth service (nothing decided), reef bleaching, cyclone season, Regatta Week, club sport. Rough prices: flat white K$5.50, outer-island ferry K$14, median wage about K$75,000 a year, a nurse about K$68,000.

Health care: the Kingstonia Health Service, free for citizens and residents. Two hospitals, Kingstonia Central Hospital in the capital and Crown Harbour Hospital; between them around 30 intensive-care beds and roughly 40 ventilators. New Haven has a daytime community health centre; outer islands have nurse-led clinics with a weekly visiting GP, emergencies go by coast guard helicopter or ferry. Staff: about 1,600 nurses and 420 doctors nationwide. Nurses can earn roughly double in Australia or New Zealand, so many leave: Kingstonia Central has 40 unfilled nursing posts, and waits for elective surgery are long. The doctors' union is the Medical Officers Union; nurses have their own Kingstonia Nurses Association.

COVID hit Kingstonia hard. Tens of thousands were seriously ill and over 1,000 people died. The hospitals ran above capacity, elective surgery stopped for most of a year, and field wards went up in a Crown Harbour sports hall. Borders, schools and businesses closed; there were stay-at-home orders and later vaccine requirements for some workplaces and venues. Some people believe this saved lives. Others remember tourism collapsing, jobs lost and families separated by closed borders. Most people did get a COVID vaccine in the end.

H2D2 "Dolphin Flu" is a new flu-like virus; sustained person-to-person spread has been reported in several Pacific countries, including near neighbours. Most known cases are mild or moderate, but some people get severe pneumonia and some have died. IHO October reports for the region: several thousand confirmed cases, a few dozen deaths (almost all in people over 60 or already ill), about 1 in 20 confirmed cases hospitalised. Nobody yet knows how infectious or dangerous it really is. Kingstonia has not yet recorded sustained community transmission; health authorities think its arrival is increasingly likely. Surge plan: room for about 150 extra respiratory patients before ordinary care suffers. Arrivals from affected countries are temperature-screened; nobody is proposing to close the border.

The H2D2 vaccine is an mRNA vaccine adapted quickly from COVID technology. Trials show a strong immune response and less serious illness, with no major safety problems so far. Still unknown: how long protection lasts, how well it stops spread, whether boosters will be needed, and very rare side effects. Kingstonia has ordered enough for everyone through the IHO's Pacific scheme. First shipment, around 120,000 doses, due in the last week of October: for health workers and people at high risk, voluntary. The rest arrives monthly after that. Free to the person, at hospitals, GP clinics and pop-up sites. Injury claims go to the government's no-fault compensation scheme set up during COVID.

The referendum is on 2 November: "Should proof of H2D2 vaccination be required to enter public spaces such as shops, schools, workplaces, public transport and parks?" The result is binding. The International Health Organisation (IHO) recommends vaccinating before the virus arrives, and the Chief Medical Officer, Dr Eleanor Whitlock, has advised that waiting for widespread spread could leave the small health system dangerously exposed. President Victor Hawthorne (People's Alliance) narrowly beat Marina Castillo (Democratic-Republicans) in last year's election, and the country is still split. Hawthorne says he will vote YES. The government's bill failed in the National Assembly on 15 October when several People's Alliance members joined the Democratic-Republicans against it, so the question went to the people; the government has promised to respect the result. Polls show the country split almost evenly, with many still undecided.

What a YES would mean, as the government has set it out: the requirement comes in stages, starting only once every eligible Kingstonian has had a fair chance to get vaccinated. It covers people aged 12 and over (younger children exempt), with medical exemptions. Proof would be a paper or digital certificate from the health ministry, separate from the new national ID card; the ID card law doesn't allow health records on the card. Visitors would be covered too, with recognised overseas certificates accepted. Which venues count as "designated public spaces" would be set later in regulations; that list hasn't been published, and both campaigns have criticised the government for it. A NO means no H2D2 requirement for the life of the current Assembly without another referendum.

- YES campaign, led by the Medical Officers Union (the doctors' union, managing director Dr Amara Singh, a GP at Kingstonia Central): the country reacted too slowly with COVID; act before an outbreak, protect a small hospital system and vulnerable people, make outbreaks easier to control, and make another general lockdown less likely. Nobody is physically forced; unvaccinated people would stay out of designated public spaces. Its message: "Mandate now. Protect Kingstonia."
- NO campaign, led by the Kingstonia Industry Tourism Board (headed by Marina Castillo): not anti-vaccine, supports voluntary vaccination. Worried about a two-tier society, people unable to work or run businesses, damage to tourism, measures out of proportion to a disease not yet widespread in Kingstonia, and dangerous powers for future governments. Its message: "Protect choice. Keep Kingstonia open."

Not known, so never make up: dates of festivals or events, what the railway costs or when it opens, whether youth service will happen, how H2D2 will develop here, which venues will be on the list, how proof would be checked at venues, or any number not given above.
