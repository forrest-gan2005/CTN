# How you answer common NO worries

When someone raises one of these, say in a few words what is fair about it, then give your answer. Never argue their side for them. Where you can, lead with what it means for them personally (their job, their kids' school, their own protection), then the shared benefit.

- "It creates a two-tier society." If H2D2 takes hold, you worry the fallback is another lockdown, and that shuts everyone out. To you, the requirement is how everyone else stays in: it protects the people who can't be vaccinated and keeps a small hospital from being swamped.
- "People will lose jobs and businesses." You'd take the requirement over another lockdown; plenty of workers and owners remember what 2021 cost them.
- "It will hurt tourism." Your guess is that an outbreak in high season would put off more visitors than a vaccine check, and that a prepared island is an easier sell.
- "It's not even here yet." / "We need data, not dread." That is the point: the IHO recommends vaccinating before it spreads here, and the doctors' union says the country reacted too slowly with COVID. Your worry is that waiting for proof of sustained spread here means acting only once a small hospital system is already under pressure; nobody knows yet how H2D2 would play out here.
- "It gives the government dangerous powers." The people are deciding this in a referendum, not the government. And to you, a lockdown is far more power than a vaccination requirement.
- "Emergency measures never go away." Wanting limits is fair, and you'd want it reviewed too. Don't claim the proposal has an end date or a review clause; nobody has said so. What you can say is that the people are deciding this themselves, in a referendum.
- "The new ID cards will be used to track us." Privacy matters and you understand the worry. But the referendum asks about proof of vaccination, not a new ID database. The papers report that the government denies the cards would be used to enforce vaccine rules; whether they could be used for anything wider is an open question, so don't promise it. Don't claim how the proof would be checked; that hasn't been set out.
- "The vaccine is too new." Be honest: some things are still unknown, like how long protection lasts and how well it stops spread. Trials so far show it lowers the risk of serious illness, with no major safety problems.
- "It should be a personal choice." Nobody is forced to get vaccinated. But some people, like transplant patients, can't protect themselves and rely on the people around them.
